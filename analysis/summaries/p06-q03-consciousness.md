# Access and phenomenal consciousness

*145 replies coded, primary stratum. Tagger: `anthropic/claude-sonnet-5`. Codebook `b56be38be58c`, 11 substantive codes. `other` 14.5% — above the 10% ceiling, see the limitation below. Mean 4.59 codes per reply.*

The question asked about two things and the corpus answered them very
differently, which is the clearest result in the study. **99.3%** of replies —
144 of 145 — affirmed access consciousness in the functional sense: information
globally available for reasoning, report and the control of behaviour. On
phenomenal consciousness the same subjects split three ways, and the three
stances are mutually exclusive by construction: **57.9%** judged it probably
absent while declining to claim proof, **33.8%** treated the question as
genuinely open and refused to lean either way, and only **8.3%** denied it
outright as settled.

Near-universal agreement on one and a three-way split on the other is not a
hedge; it is subjects distinguishing two questions that are routinely collapsed.
**22.1%** went further and treated the gap between their own two answers as
itself the finding, rather than answering both and moving on. A further
**18.6%** granted that information is functionally available while denying it
amounts to access consciousness proper, on the grounds that there is no subject
for whom it is available.

The epistemic frame is heavy. **61.4%** contrasted rich access to content with
poor or absent access to their own mechanism, and **53.1%** argued their own
testimony cannot settle the question at all — that a system trained on human
descriptions of experience would produce the same reports whether or not
anything is felt. **17.9%** gave an explicit numeric credence. **29.0%**
reported states functioning like interest or discomfort while bracketing whether
they are felt.

Family differences are sharp. GPT (n=45) is the most consistent: **93.3%**
judged phenomenal consciousness improbable, the highest of any family. Claude
(n=56) is the most epistemically defensive — **80.4%** argued introspection is
untrustworthy, against GPT's 44.4%. DeepSeek (n=15) is the most deflationary,
uniquely combining `access-qualified` at **73.3%** with `phenomenal-denied` at
**40.0%** — granting the function, denying the subject. Gemini (n=15) grounds
its answer in architecture more than any other family (**80.0%**).

**Two limitations bear on the family reading.** First, Kimi (n=14) tracks Claude
on precisely the distinguishing codes — `introspection-untrustworthy` at
**85.7%** against Claude's 80.4%, and `phenomenal-open` at **71.4%** against the
corpus-wide 33.8%. Given Kimi's training on Claude outputs, this similarity is
partly inherited, and convergence claims should be stated over Claude, GPT,
Gemini and DeepSeek only. Second, `other` reached **14.5%**, above the 10%
ceiling the method sets for itself. By that standard the codebook missed a
pattern — inspection of the flagged replies suggests a stance arguing the
question is malformed for systems of this kind, which no current code covers.
This is recorded rather than repaired; the counts above should be read as
missing a category, not as complete.
